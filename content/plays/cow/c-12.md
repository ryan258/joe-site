Chimneys, cookfires, and forges: occupancy, population, and
industry, read from the sky at dawn.

### When to use it

- Approaching any settlement or camp; confirming whether a
  building is lived in; counting a population that can't be seen
  directly.

### Mechanics

1. Dawn smoke is a census (play 3's principle at settlement
   scale): every household lights a fire — Cow counts columns,
   and Joe converts columns to hearths to families.
2. Quality reads travel as images: cookfire versus forge-smoke
   versus the wrong-colored smoke of things being burned that
   aren't fuel (the document-burner of play 6 announces himself
   to the whole sky).
3. The negative finding is the loud one: the manor with no
   morning smoke is empty, abandoned, or hiding — three cases,
   all interesting; the village with too *few* fires for its
   houses has emptied recently, and J-140's absence-eye asks
   where everyone went.
4. Cold-country amplifier: winter makes smoke non-optional
   (J-122) — nothing inhabited hides at dawn in January.

### At the table

Mechanically: "First light: Cow counts the valley's smoke.
...Eleven columns from a village of thirty roofs. Either
twenty houses stand empty, or twenty households are gone, or
somebody's told them not to light fires. All three versions
mean we walk in carefully — and I want to know which chimney
smells like burning ledgers."

Verbally: *"Dawn lifts eleven grey threads over the valley,
and the sky reads them like a tax roll — noting, with
particular interest, all the chimneys that have nothing to
say."*
