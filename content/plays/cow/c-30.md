Dropped where the right person will pick it up: the insertion
that lets the target carry the sensor home themselves.

### When to use it

- Targets whose homes and routes can't be approached: the
  careful suspect, the guarded courier — people who can be
  *met* only by accident.

### Mechanics

1. The drop is staged on the target's known route (plays 8–9
   supply the route intelligence): the toy sits where their
   specific habits collide with it — the bench they always
   take, the doorway their cart passes, the pew they favor.
2. Human selection does the vetting: people who pocket a
   lost toy are keeping it (for a child, usually — the play
   often becomes play 28 by accident) or selling it (becomes
   play 29); either way it travels *inward* through their
   life, carried past every defense they own.
3. The tracking problem is the play's real cost: beyond 100
   feet the toy reports nothing live — so the drop pairs
   with a tail (J-138, Cow can't tail itself) or with
   patience (the standing brief: *watch, remember, and fly
   home at the third dawn* — play 25's grammar with a clock).
4. The recovery is the con's second act: Joe the scholar,
   inquiring door to door after his lost keepsake, reward
   offered — a cover story that *also* interviews the
   target's household on the way in (play 78's warmth,
   weaponized gently).

### At the table

Mechanically: "She takes the same bench every market-day.
The toy's on it at dawn, scuffed and rain-damp — lost, not
placed. If she pockets it, it rides home through a door
we've never gotten near, watches for two days, and flies
home the third dawn. Then I come asking after my lost cow,
door to door, reward in hand, and her neighbors tell me
all about her while helping me look."

Verbally: *"The courier checks her tail, her locks, her
letters — everything she carries on purpose. It never
occurs to her to suspect the small sad toy she rescued
from a bench, which is riding her charity straight through
her own front door."*
